From a sketch to something you can cast

Design process  ·  Burnaby, B.C.  ·  10 business days

Most custom work dies somewhere between the drawing and the bench — a beautiful render that will not cast, a head that cannot be set, a shank that polishes away to nothing. This is the route a piece actually takes here, step by step, so you know what you are buying before you buy it.

The route ■

01

You send what you have

A sketch on the back of a receipt is enough. So is a photograph of a ring the customer already owns, a written description, or a stone on its way with its measurements written down. Nothing has to be drawn to scale and nothing has to be named correctly — if you cannot remember whether it is a cathedral or a trellis, describe it and we will find it.

If a 3D file already exists, that is the other service: I check it and print it, without redesigning anything. See castable patterns.

02

The piece gets built in 3D

This is where most of the work lives, and almost none of it shows in a render. Prong seats, stone seats, wall thickness and sprue placement are settled on the screen — not discovered at the bench three weeks later with a torch in hand.

I model this way because I have worked at the bench. A model built by someone who has never set a stone will look correct and fight you the whole way through setting. The difference does not appear in a picture; it appears in how much rework the piece needs after it comes out of the flask.

03

Two rounds of revisions

Two rounds are included in every design price. Thinner shank, taller gallery, a head moved two millimetres, a stone sitting lower, a different shoulder — that is what the rounds are for, and you are not billed for using them.

Most pieces settle inside the two. If a design turns into a genuinely different piece halfway through, I will say so before doing the work rather than after.

04

The castability check

Included in every design price, and run before anything is printed. What gets looked at:

  • wall thickness, so nothing disappears under the polisher
  • prong seats and stone seats, so the stones you have actually sit in them
  • sprue placement, so the metal reaches the far end of the piece
  • trapped volumes and closed cavities, where investment cannot get out

None of this is glamorous. It is the difference between a pattern that casts first time and one that comes back as a lesson.

05

The pattern gets printed

Patterns print at 25 µm layers in a wax-filled castable resin the manufacturer rates at 0.03% ash, with low thermal expansion and shrinkage. Fine detail — milgrain, engraving, bead work — survives the print, and the burnout leaves the flask clean.

The technical side of that choice is written up separately in castable patterns, in detail.

06

It arrives at your bench

Free by hand anywhere in Metro Vancouver — I would rather put the pattern in your hand and let you judge the surface yourself than send you a photograph of it. Elsewhere in British Columbia it is a $20 flat rate, tracked; $22 to the other western provinces and $30 east.

From there it is yours. Casting, setting and finishing stay at your bench — I do not take that work away from the shops I work with. If you would rather have the finished piece, local masters cast and polish it, and that is quoted per piece.
