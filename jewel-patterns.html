What you are actually buying ■

01

A pattern is a thing designed to disappear

Everything else in jewellery manufacturing is made to last. A pattern is made to burn out completely and leave a clean void behind. That is the whole job, and every property worth arguing about follows from it: how much residue is left in the flask, whether the material pushes outward on the investment as it heats, and how much of the surface detail survives into the metal.

A pattern that looks perfect on the bench and leaves grey residue in the cavity has failed. So has one that cracks the investment on its way out. Neither failure is visible when you accept delivery.

02

The resin: wax-filled, castable, 0.03% ash

Patterns here print in a wax-filled castable resin. The manufacturer rates it at 0.03% ash — that is the fraction of the pattern’s own mass still sitting in your flask when burnout finishes.

Why it matters more than it sounds: ash does not distribute itself politely. It collects in the finest parts of the cavity — prong tips, bead work, the undercut of an engraving — which is precisely where a casting is judged. The same resin is rated for low thermal expansion and shrinkage, which is the other half of the problem: a pattern that swells before it softens is what splits investment and ruins a whole flask rather than one piece.

This is why the figure is printed on this page instead of being left to goodwill. If you cast someone else’s patterns for a living, it is the number you would ask for first.

03

25 µm layers, and what they are worth

Twenty-five microns is a quarter of the thickness of a sheet of paper. At that height, stepping on a shallow curve stops being something you can feel with a fingernail, and the features that usually suffer first survive the print:

  • milgrain beading, which turns into a smooth rope at coarser layers
  • hand-engraving and relief, where the step lines read as tool chatter
  • bright-cut edges and the inside walls of bead settings
  • prong tips, which blunt before anything else does

Finer layers are possible on most machines and are mostly a way of selling print time. The honest statement is that 25 µm is the point past which the investment surface, not the pattern, becomes the limit on what you can see in the cast metal.

04

You cast it. In your own furnace.

Patterns leave here cleaned and ready to sprue. Burnout stays yours, on your own schedule, in your own kiln — I do not ask anyone to change a burnout cycle that already works for them, and I do not cast on your behalf unless you ask for a finished piece.

That division is deliberate. Casting, setting and finishing stay at your bench. I make the pattern; the craft after the flask is yours, and I am not interested in quietly competing with the shops I supply.

05

Every file gets checked before it prints

Print-only orders still get a castability check, at no extra cost, because a pattern that cannot cast is a waste of both our time. What gets looked at is the same list as for my own designs — wall thickness, prong and stone seats, sprue placement, and any volume that investment cannot drain out of.

If something is going to cause trouble I tell you before printing and say what I would change. I do not silently alter your model; it is your design.

06

Sending a file, and what it costs

Send whatever your CAD produces, along with the finger size or finished dimensions and anything you want held exactly. If the format is unusual, ask first and I will tell you straight away whether it opens cleanly.

Print-only pricing goes by size — $35 small, $55 medium, $75 large — and repeats of the same model come down steeply: 15% off from two, 25% from five, 35% from ten. Print-only orders dispatch within three business days. Metro Vancouver delivery is free and by hand; elsewhere in B.C. it is a $20 tracked flat rate, $22 to the other western provinces, $30 east. Everything is on the rates page.

If you want the design done as well as the pattern, that route is written up in from a sketch to something you can cast.
